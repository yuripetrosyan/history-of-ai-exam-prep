1. Real time computing — Whirlwind, SAGE, SABRE. Why "real time" mattered. ▸
B-question both yearsAsked in 2025 I & II.

Real time computing means the machine processes input fast enough to react to events as they happen, instead of running batch jobs overnight. The book frames Whirlwind as the breakthrough. Jay Forrester at MIT started Whirlwind in the 1940s as a flight simulator project, but it was repeatedly close to cancellation. Perry Crawford saved it twice. First he reframed it as a digital project rather than analogue, which kept the navy interested. Second, after the Soviets tested an atomic bomb in 1949, Crawford pushed Whirlwind as the answer to the new air-defence problem.

That pivot became SAGE, the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, finished around 1958. SAGE was the first large real time system: radar data flowed into the computer, operators saw aircraft on screens, and the machine could direct interceptors. The civilian spin-off was SABRE (1964), American Airlines' seat reservation system, built by IBM using the SAGE blueprint. It let agents anywhere book a seat instantly.

The historical point is twofold. Technically, real time forced new ideas: magnetic core memory, light pens, large duplex systems with backup. Institutionally, the Cold War paid for the research, then airlines and banks appropriated the technology for civilian use. This is the prof's "appropriation" pattern again: a military problem became commercial infrastructure. Without real time computing there would be no airline booking, no ATMs, and no interactive operating systems later in the 1960s.

2. Software crisis — OS/360 as exemplar. Brooks vs Dijkstra. ▸
B-question both years2025 I asked "real crisis?" 2025 II asked about OS/360.

The "software crisis" is the term used at the 1968 NATO conference in Garmisch to describe how software projects in the 1960s kept missing deadlines, going over budget, and shipping with bugs. IBM's OS/360 is the textbook exemplar. It was the operating system for the System/360 family of machines announced in 1964. Fred Brooks led the project. It eventually employed around a thousand programmers, cost roughly 500 million dollars, and was delivered late and unstable.

Brooks wrote about it afterwards in The Mythical Man-Month (1975). His key insight: adding more programmers to a late project makes it later, because communication costs grow faster than productivity. He also argued software is "intrinsically" hard because it has to handle every edge case in an imagined world.

Dijkstra took a different view. He thought the crisis was not intrinsic but a failure of discipline. Programmers were sloppy, used GOTO statements, and did not write code that could be reasoned about. His response was structured programming and formal methods.

Together the two responses framed software engineering as a real discipline. The lecturer's point is that OS/360 was big enough and public enough to make this debate visible. It exposed that writing software at industrial scale was nothing like the small-team programming of the 1950s, and the field had to invent management techniques and ways of thinking that did not exist yet.

3. Computer manufacturers reconstructed. The 1950s pivot to business. ▸
B-question both years2025 I & II both featured this theme.

In the late 1940s, computers were one-off scientific instruments. By the late 1950s they were business machines you could buy from a catalogue. Campbell-Kelly tracks how the early computer industry was reconstructed around that shift.

The first commercial computer was UNIVAC, built by Eckert and Mauchly and delivered to the US Census Bureau in 1951. UNIVAC was famous for predicting Eisenhower's 1952 election victory on live television, which sold the public on the idea of computers. But Eckert and Mauchly were not businessmen. Remington Rand bought them, and even with UNIVAC's head start they lost the market.

The winner was IBM. IBM had the punched-card customer base from the 1930s — banks, insurance companies, government — and a feared sales force trained by Thomas Watson Sr. When IBM finally released the 701 (1952) and then the 650 (1954), they used existing customer relationships to push computers into offices. By 1956 IBM had passed Remington Rand. By the late 1950s, IBM controlled around 70% of the market.

One sub-theme is magnetic tape. Early manufacturers struggled with reliability, head wear, and standardization. UNIVAC used metal tape; IBM used plastic-based tape that was lighter and easier to handle.

The takeaway: the early industry was reconstructed by sales, support, and existing customer trust, not by who had the best machine. Technology was necessary but not sufficient.

4. IBM 1401 — good technology + great marketing. Campbell-Kelly's line of thought. ▸
B-question both yearsWatson framing (May) and 1401 specifically (July).

The IBM 1401, released in 1959, was a mid-range business computer that became one of the most successful machines of its era. By 1964 about 10,000 had been installed worldwide. Campbell-Kelly explains the success as a combination of solid technology and IBM's marketing engine.

The technology side: the 1401 used transistors instead of vacuum tubes, which made it smaller, cheaper, and more reliable than earlier business computers. It came with a chain printer (the 1403) that was a real engineering achievement, printing 600 lines a minute. The system was modular, so customers could buy a basic configuration and add memory or peripherals later. For most companies the 1401 was the first computer that actually fit their workflow rather than forcing them to redesign processes around it.

The marketing side: IBM did not just sell the box. They sold the whole package — installation, training, financing, and ongoing service contracts. The sales force was trained at IBM's own school. They knew their customers' industries and could talk to managers in business language, not technical language. IBM also offered the 1401 on a lease, which spread the cost and locked customers in.

Campbell-Kelly's point is that this is why IBM, not the technologically interesting competitors, came to dominate. The 1401 was not the most advanced computer in 1959 but it was the one a credit-union manager in Iowa could actually buy and use. Good enough technology plus great service is the formula.

5. Timesharing — new ways of computing in the 1960s. CTSS, DTSS, BASIC. ▸
B-question both yearsDiscuss at least two new ways of computing.

In the early 1960s, computers ran in batch mode: you submitted a deck of punched cards, waited hours, and got a printout. Two "new ways" broke that model and set up personal computing.

The first was timesharing. The idea was that one big computer could serve many users at once by giving each a small slice of CPU time. CTSS (Compatible Time Sharing System) at MIT, demonstrated in 1961 under Fernando Corbató, was the first real implementation. Suddenly a programmer could sit at a teletype, type code, and see results in seconds. CTSS led to Project MAC and eventually Multics, which Unix later imitated and simplified.

The second was BASIC and the Dartmouth Time Sharing System (DTSS). In 1964, John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz at Dartmouth designed BASIC as a language any student could learn in an afternoon, running on a campus-wide timesharing system. Within a few years a majority of Dartmouth students had written a program. This was the moment computing escaped the priesthood of professional programmers.

Campbell-Kelly's argument is that these new ways prepared people for the personal computer of the 1970s. By the time the Altair and the Apple II appeared, there was already a generation that had typed at a keyboard, seen output appear on a screen or a printer, and knew what a programming language felt like. Without timesharing and BASIC, the PC would have arrived to a public that did not know what to do with it. The hardware revolution rode on a behavioural one.

6. Broadening the appeal of the PC. 1980s — GUI, CD-ROM, failed challengers. ▸
B-question both yearsWhy is it relevant to know about the failures?

The early 1980s personal computer was a hobby and an office tool. By the early 1990s it had become a household appliance. Campbell-Kelly calls this "broadening the appeal." Three things drove it.

The first was the graphical user interface. Xerox PARC had built the first GUI in the 1970s, but it was Apple's Macintosh (1984) and then Microsoft Windows (Windows 3.0 in 1990) that made it mass-market. Suddenly people who would never type a command could use a computer.

The second was multimedia. The CD-ROM, introduced in the late 1980s, let publishers ship games, encyclopaedias, and educational software on a single disk. Myst (1993) is the standard example. This made the PC something a family would buy for the children, not just an office machine.

The third was networking, which arrives in the mid-1990s with the web.

The exam often asks about the failed GUI companies. VisiCorp (VisiOn, 1983) and GEM (Digital Research, 1985) both released GUI systems for the PC before Microsoft did. Both failed: VisiOn was too expensive and slow; GEM lost a lawsuit from Apple about lookalike. Why does it matter that we know about them? Because it shows the winners were not technologically inevitable. The market was contested, and the historians' job is to recover the alternatives. Knowing about the failures is what stops history from being a victory narrative dictated by Microsoft and Apple.

7. Internet — three desires + email sociology. Why people built it. ▸
B-question both yearsThree desires (May) and email sociology (July).

Campbell-Kelly explains the internet not as one project but as three desires that all wanted the same infrastructure. The desires came from different communities and converged.

The first desire was military. The Cold War context made the US Department of Defense want a network that could keep working after parts of it were destroyed. ARPANET (1969) used packet switching designed by Paul Baran at RAND so that messages could find alternative routes.

The second desire was academic. Computer scientists wanted to share data and software across universities. ARPANET grew from four nodes in 1969 to dozens by the mid-1970s, and email turned out to be the most-used application within a year, even though it was almost an afterthought.

The third desire was civic and commercial. By the late 1980s the National Science Foundation took over the backbone (NSFNET), commercial providers arrived, and the World Wide Web (Berners-Lee, CERN, 1989–91) finally made the system usable for non-experts.

The email part of the question. Sociologists and psychologists in the 1970s and 1980s noticed that email had effects nobody designed. People flamed each other, friendships formed between strangers across continents, hierarchies in companies flattened because a junior engineer could write to a director. The medium itself shaped behaviour. The prof's framing is that email is the first case where computer history becomes social history. We cannot understand the internet just as a technical artefact: it is also a social experiment that nobody set up on purpose.

8. Computing and governance — US vs European privacy. Compare two legal programs. ▸
B-question both yearsB12 both years.

The US and Europe took very different paths on computing-related privacy. The lecture frames this as a "computing and governance" question and asks us to compare two legal programs.

The European program is the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), in force from 2018 across the EU. GDPR treats personal data as a fundamental right under EU law. Companies must have a lawful basis to process data, must allow users to access and delete their data, and face fines up to 4% of global revenue for breaches. It is comprehensive and applies to any company doing business in the EU. The roots go back further: the 1981 Council of Europe Convention 108 and individual national laws in Germany and Sweden from the 1970s.

The US program is sectoral and patchier. HIPAA (1996) regulates health data. COPPA (1998) regulates children's data online. CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act, 2018) is the closest the US has to GDPR but only applies in one state. There is no federal omnibus privacy law.

The contrast is philosophical. Europe came out of a 20th century where governments had used data to persecute people — the legacy of fascism and East German surveillance — and treats privacy as a defence against the state and large companies. The US, with a stronger first-amendment tradition and a tech industry it wanted to grow, treated data as a commercial asset and intervened only sector by sector.

The historical point is that the same digital infrastructure produced two opposite regulatory cultures because each culture asked a different question about what computers are for.

9. AI and education. Two examples — PI and LOGO. ▸
A-question both yearsA5 May 2025 + A5 July 2025.

Two examples from the lectures illustrate how AI met education. They come from opposite ends of the educational philosophy spectrum.

The first is programmed instruction. The behaviourist B. F. Skinner argued in 1954 that learning could be broken into small steps, each reinforced by a correct answer. This became the foundation of the PLATO project at the University of Illinois from 1960. PLATO offered drill-style tutoring on a network of terminals: the computer asked a question, the student answered, the computer either moved on or gave a hint. By the 1970s, PLATO ran tutoring software in chemistry, languages, and music for tens of thousands of students.

The second is LOGO, designed by Seymour Papert with Marvin Minsky at MIT in 1967 and popularised in Papert's book Mindstorms (1981). LOGO was the opposite philosophy. Instead of drilling students with right answers, it gave them a "turtle" they could program. The child made the turtle move and draw, learned debugging by fixing their own mistakes, and discovered geometry from the inside out. Papert called this "powerful ideas" learning.

The Dutch case is worth mentioning. In 1968 a state report on "computer wiskunde" called for teaching algorithms in schools. C. J. Vonk in 1971 wrote a teaching algorithm based on blind-dating to introduce arrays. A. van der Sluis in 1969 argued that teaching algorithms gave citizens a defence against a "big brother" society.

The historical point: AI and education was not just a tool question, it was an argument about what kind of citizen the future needed.

10. Defining technology (Bolter). Two 21st-century AI examples. ▸
C-question both yearsC5 both years. 1960s cybernetics (May) vs 21st century AI (July).

Jay David Bolter, in Turing's Man (1984), introduced the idea of a defining technology. A defining technology is one a culture uses as the central metaphor to understand itself. Bolter argued that the clock defined the Newtonian universe, the steam engine defined the industrial age, and the computer defines the late 20th century. The point is not that the technology causes the culture, but that we cannot stop ourselves from describing the mind, the body, society, and even nature in the vocabulary of whatever the dominant machine is.

Two 21st-century examples of AI as a defining technology.

The first is healthcare. Hospitals and policymakers now describe medical decision-making in AI terms. We "diagnose" with classifiers. We talk about "training" doctors on data sets. IBM's Watson Health was sold to hospitals around 2014 as a system that would read journal articles and recommend treatments. Even though Watson Health largely failed and was sold off in 2022, the metaphor stuck. Medicine reframed itself as a pattern-recognition problem because AI was the defining technology of the decade.

The second is justice and policing. Predictive policing tools like COMPAS (criminal risk-scoring in US courts) and city-level systems that predict crime rates have entered the legal system. Judges are now asked to weigh an algorithm's recommendation. This is exactly Bolter's claim: a domain that used to think in moral terms now thinks in classification terms because AI provides the dominant vocabulary.

The historical point is that AI does not just do new things; it changes the language a society uses to describe what it already does.

11. Histories of AI — appropriation and agendas. Alternative starting points. ▸
C-question both yearsC1 both years. Appropriation (July) + starting points/agendas (May).

The traditional history of computing centres on a few canonical starting points — ENIAC 1946, Dartmouth 1956 for AI — and presents the field as a clean march of progress. The prof argues this is a chosen story serving an agenda, and that other starting points are equally defensible.

For AI the textbook starting point is the Dartmouth summer workshop of 1956, where McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, and Shannon coined "artificial intelligence." This is the canonical origin. But Pamela McCorduck's Machines Who Think (1979) deliberately pushed the start back to ancient mythology — the Golem, Talos — to make AI feel like an old human dream. That is one alternative.

A second alternative is the Macy conferences (1946–53) on cybernetics, where Norbert Wiener, McCulloch, and Pitts already had a working theory of mind and machine before "AI" had a name. The Dartmouth group later marketed cybernetics' problems as their own.

A third is Babbage's Analytical Engine (1830s onwards), which Ada Lovelace described as capable of representing anything formal, including music. If we treat that as the start, AI is nearly two centuries old.

This is what the lecturer means by appropriation: each generation chooses a starting point that justifies its current research programme. Dartmouth gave AI its symbolic-logic agenda. McCorduck's mythological framing gave AI cultural authority during the funding-poor 1970s. The lesson: histories of AI are tools used to defend present-day claims. Reflecting on the choice of origin is itself part of the history.

12. AI winters and agendas — McCorduck context. Why winters keep happening. ▸
C/bonus both yearsC6 May 2025 + NO BONUS July 2025.

An AI winter is a period when AI funding and public interest collapse after a wave of hype fails to deliver. There have been two main winters: roughly 1974–80 after the Lighthill report in the UK declared general AI research a dead end, and roughly 1987–93 after the expert-systems bubble burst.

The lecturer pairs the AI winters with Pamela McCorduck's Machines Who Think (1979), and the pairing is the key. McCorduck wrote her book at the bottom of the first winter. She traced AI back to myth and ancient automata to give the field cultural depth at a moment when its scientific claims were under attack. Her book was not just a history. It was an argument that AI research deserved to continue, even if the current generation had over-promised.

This is the lecturer's claim about agendas. Histories of AI tend to be written when the field is under pressure, and the histories are designed to defend present-day funding. McCorduck's history rebuilt the field's public legitimacy. The cyberneticists in the 1950s wrote histories that put themselves at the centre. Today's deep-learning advocates write histories that make neural networks the inevitable winner over symbolic AI.

The historical lesson from the winters themselves is that AI breakthroughs are followed by inflated promises, which collapse when the technology cannot scale. Each winter ended when a new approach (expert systems in the 1980s, deep learning around 2012) made the old failures look like a different problem. The pattern suggests we should expect another winter, and another rewritten history to follow it.

Acronyms in 1950s computing — functions of. ▸
New essay below2025 II A3.

In the 1950s, computer projects almost universally gave themselves acronyms. The Dutch were famous for this: ARRA, ZEBRA, PETER, PASCAL, MIRACLE. The American and British scene had ENIAC, UNIVAC, EDVAC, EDSAC, LEO. The lecturer pointed out three real functions, not just stylistic ones.

The first function was identity. Small computer teams, often working in universities with tiny budgets, needed a way to talk about their machine and present it to funders. An acronym gave the project a name that fit on a poster and a grant application.

The second function was memorability. The full name of ZEBRA in Dutch translates to "very simple binary calculator." Nobody would say that out loud. Saying "ZEBRA" turned a clunky technical project into something a listener could remember and repeat.

The third function, especially in the Dutch case, was humour and self-deprecation. ZEBRA literally meant "very simple": the engineers were claiming modesty even as they were building something hard. MIRACLE at Shell was openly tongue-in-cheek.

Acronyms were a small marker of how the 1950s computing community saw itself: a network of named projects, each with a memorable label, each in conversation with the others. In a period before the industry was dominated by IBM, the acronym was how a Dutch project signalled it was part of the global story.

Technology and trust (late 19th / early 20th c) — how trust was established. ▸
New essay below2025 I A1.

Before people could trust computers, they first had to trust machines in general. That trust was built up between roughly 1880 and 1930 through specific developments.

The first thing was infrastructure success. Telegraph networks crossed continents reliably from the 1860s. Railways ran on timetables. Electricity arrived in households in the late 1800s and just kept working. Each of these systems taught the public that machines could be depended on.

The second thing was office and factory mechanisation. Hollerith's punched-card machines processed the 1890 US census faster and more accurately than the human clerks who had done the 1880 one. Taylor's scientific management broke down factory work into measured motions. Ford's moving assembly line in 1913 produced standardised cars at a price ordinary people could afford. These workplaces taught a generation that mechanical systems outperformed traditional human ones.

The third thing was the cultural shift around what counted as modern. By the 1920s, "scientific" and "modern" had become positive words. To trust a machine was to be progressive. To distrust one was to be a Luddite.

By the time the first computers arrived in the 1940s, the audience was already prepared. The hard work of establishing trust in machines had been done by railways, telegraph offices, factories and assembly lines decades earlier. Computers inherited that trust.

Failed GUI companies (Apple Lisa, MS Bob, etc.) — why know about these? ▸
New essay below2025 II B8.

The graphical user interface we use every day was not first launched by Apple or Microsoft. It was launched by Xerox and then by Apple Lisa, both of which commercially failed. Knowing about these failures is what makes the eventual Mac and Windows success understandable, not inevitable.

Xerox Star (1981) used everything we now recognise as a modern computer: mouse, windows, icons, the desktop metaphor. Almost all of these had been developed at Xerox PARC by Alan Kay and others during the 1970s. The problem was the price. The Star cost about $16,000 in 1981. It was aimed at corporate office buyers, but it was too expensive for most departments, and Xerox's sales team did not really know how to sell a computer. The Star was withdrawn within a few years.

Apple Lisa (1983), launched for about $10,000, was Apple's first attempt at a consumer GUI computer. Steve Jobs had visited Xerox PARC in 1979 and taken the ideas back to Apple. The Lisa had a beautiful interface and a serious operating system, but it was slow and expensive. It also flopped commercially. What rescued the GUI for Apple was the Macintosh (1984), at $2,495.

Knowing these failed stories matters for two reasons. First, it shows that innovation by itself is not enough: price, timing and a sales channel are what made the Mac work where the Lisa did not. Second, it removes the temptation to read the history of computing as inevitable. Xerox could easily have been the Apple of the 1980s if it had priced the Star differently.

Magnetic tape problems — 1950s manufacturer struggles. ▸
New essay below2025 II B2.

In the late 1940s and early 1950s, magnetic tape was the technology that promised to replace punched cards for mass storage on computers. Eckert and Mauchly's UNIVAC, delivered in 1951, was the first commercial computer to depend on it. But getting magnetic tape to actually work in practice turned out to be harder than expected, and the manufacturers faced three kinds of problem.

The first was mechanical. Tape had to move past a read/write head at precise speeds, then start and stop suddenly between records. Early tape drives stretched the tape, snapped it, or simply lost track of where they were on the reel. UNIVAC machines were often offline because the tape drives were broken.

The second was about the material itself. Early plastic-backed tapes were sensitive to heat and humidity. They stretched in storage, which corrupted data. The metal oxide coating that held the magnetic information could flake off when the tape was rewound too many times.

The third was cultural and commercial. IBM, the dominant manufacturer, had its whole business built around punched cards. Customers were trained to think in card decks. Switching to magnetic tape meant retraining clerks, redesigning data layouts, and trusting a fragile new medium. IBM was slow to adopt tape and continued building punched-card machinery well into the 1950s. UNIVAC's lead in tape did not translate into a lasting commercial advantage.

By the late 1950s, the mechanical, material and cultural problems had all been solved enough for tape to become standard. But the first decade was a struggle.

💡 Key concepts (the prof's vocabulary)

Drop these terms into any essay. They are the lecturer's signature language.

Three Traditions

Administration, process control, science. Computing has three histories, not one. They converged in the modern computer.

Appropriation

One field's technology gets repurposed by another (Cold War → airlines, military → academic networks). Use this whenever a question asks how history connects across domains.

Agendas

Histories of computing/AI are written to defend present-day funding. McCorduck 1979 is the textbook case.

Defining technology (Bolter)

Every age has one machine it uses to describe itself: clock → steam engine → computer → AI. Bolter, Turing's Man, 1984.

Three desires (Internet)

Military, academic, and civic/commercial communities all wanted the same packet-switched infrastructure for different reasons.

Software crisis

NATO conference, Garmisch, 1968. Big software projects (OS/360) kept failing. Born of OS/360 chaos. Brooks vs Dijkstra response.

Stored-program concept

Von Neumann's First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC (1945). Program and data share memory — the foundational idea of the modern computer.

Moore School Lectures

Summer 1946 at UPenn. Eight-week school taught stored-program design to Wilkes (EDSAC) and others — gateway to Europe.

Drosophila of AI

Chess as AI's lab fruit-fly. But the analogy meant fixation, not testbed — Deep Blue 1997 won chess without delivering general intelligence.

AI winter

Periods (1974–80, 1987–93) when AI funding collapsed after hype outran results. Pattern repeats; expect another.

Bluffing your way in (Alberts)

How small Dutch teams (ARRA, ZEBRA) bootstrapped a national computer industry on tiny budgets.

Data as terra/animo nullius

Jonnie Penn 2023: data colonialism — companies treat user data like "land that belongs to no one." Reading list item.

Computing for its own sake?

Campbell-Kelly on Moore School: "computing was not performed for its own sake, but always as a means to an end." Computing is utilitarian.

Broadening the appeal

The 1980s shift: PC moves from office to home through GUI, CD-ROM multimedia, then networking.

Electronic brain metaphor

1949 Berkeley's Giant Brains + tabloid press. A sales metaphor that set unrealistic AI expectations and seeded later winters.

Company culture

The prof's reminder that IBM's sales force, Xerox PARC's culture, or Apple's Jobs/Wozniak dynamic shape outcomes as much as engineering.

Writing Toolkit

Phrases, hooks, and structure that work in almost any History of AI essay. Use these to write smoother answers and to extend word count without padding.

🎯 The prof's body structure

From the seminar PDF — this is the actual essay template the lecturer uses to teach you.

For any question, decompose the prompt into clauses (a, b, c, d…) and answer each in turn. Example with the ENIAC question:

  • a) ENIAC is an obvious starting point because…
  • b) An alternative starting point would be…
  • c) It counts as an alternative because…
  • d) It was passed by because…

Always re-read the question and break it into pieces before you start the body.

🪝 Opening hooks (pick one)

  • "When we look back at [X], it's tempting to imagine a single straight line — but the real story is messier."
  • "[X] is often called [Y]. The label is convenient, but the story behind it is more interesting."
  • "It seems strange to say that [counter-intuitive claim], but on closer inspection..."
  • "In [year], [event]. With this, [thing] began — but the story behind it stretches back much further."
  • "[X] looks like a recent phenomenon. Its roots reach back to [period]."

Avoid clichés like "Throughout history…" or "Since the dawn of time…"

🔗 Transition phrases

Use these to glue paragraphs together so your essay flows.

  • By contrast, / However,
  • Crucially, / More importantly,
  • What this reveals is…
  • The deeper question is…
  • Equally important,
  • It is significant that…
  • This is not to say that…

🔍 Concrete-example phrases

Graders reward specific evidence — names, dates, places. Lead in with these:

  • Consider the case of ___ in ___ (year).
  • A telling instance is ___.
  • Take, for example, ___.
  • An illustrative case is ___.
  • The clearest example is ___.

Two specific examples beat five vague ones.

💭 Reflective phrases (the prof loves these)

The course is "not prescriptive but reflective." Use phrasing that thinks about history, not just describes it.

  • This raises the question of…
  • From a sociological angle,…
  • Read this way, the technology is not just…
  • The implication is that…
  • This is not just a technical change but a cultural one.
  • Knowing this changes how we read [present-day phenomenon].

🎬 Conclusion templates

  • "Taken together, [evidence] shows that [argument]. The deeper lesson is [reflective point]."
  • "[X] is best understood not as [simple framing] but as [complex framing]."
  • "Knowing this history changes how we read [present-day technology] today."
  • "The lesson is that [X] was never just [surface description] — it was always also [deeper claim]."

A good conclusion always says something the intro didn't — adds, not just repeats.

🏛️ The Three Traditions hook

The prof's signature framework. Drop it into any computing-history question and you can usually add 100 words plus look like you understood the lecture.

  • Administration → money, urgency, paperwork. Hollerith, IBM, NCR, typewriters.
  • Process control → analogue tech, industrial outlines. Shell 1935, Edison 1898, DSM 1956.
  • Science & engineering → concepts, calculations. Babbage, Bush 1934, Hartree, Tinbergen, Manhattan.

Phrase: "From the administrative tradition, …" / "The science and engineering tradition meanwhile…" — instantly adds structure.

🧠 The two AI prehistory threads

Use these whenever a question is about AI's origins:

  • Logic / Language → Boole 1854 → Frege 1879 → Russell & Whitehead 1910–13 → Hilbert 1928 → Gödel 1931 → Church & Turing 1936.
  • Human thinking & behaviour → Descartes → Jevons → Torres Quevedo (chess 1912) → fiction (Frankenstein 1818, RUR 1920, Metropolis 1927) → cybernetics.

Both threads predate ENIAC (1946). That's why "AI is older than computing."

📏 Word-count extension (without padding)

Stuck at 200 words? Add depth, not filler. Try one of these:

  • Counter-point. "One might object that… However, …"
  • Specify date + place. "In 1890, in Washington, …" beats "Eventually…"
  • Add a "so what". After every example, write one sentence on why it matters.
  • European angle. The book is American; the prof loves a European parallel (Babbage, Turing, Boole, Frege, Bletchley, Zuse, Esperanto, Welby, Klavarscribo).
  • Quote or paraphrase. "As Babbage wrote in 1832, computing was 'the division of mental labor.'"
  • Connect to today. One sentence at the end linking the history to a present-day phenomenon.

🚫 Words and phrases to avoid

  • "In conclusion" — try "Taken together" or "What this shows is".
  • "Throughout history" — too vague.
  • "Many people believe" — who?
  • "Since the dawn of time" — never.
  • "A lot of" — say "many" or, better, give a number.
  • "Things" / "stuff" — be specific.
  • "Very important" — show, don't tell.

📐 Essay scaffold (copy this)

Intro (~50–80 words).

  • One-sentence hook.
  • One-sentence summary of your argument.
  • Optional: signpost ("I'll do this in three parts…").

Body (~150–350 words).

  • Decompose question into clauses.
  • One paragraph per clause.
  • Each paragraph: claim → concrete example (name + year) → "so what".

Conclusion (~40–70 words).

  • Don't repeat — synthesise.
  • One reflective sentence (philosophical / sociological angle).
  • Optional: connect to today.

⚠️ Common traps

  • Narrating ≠ arguing. Don't just list events — say what they mean.
  • Generic claims. "Computers changed everything" — say what, when, for whom.
  • Ignoring half the prompt. If the question has two parts, answer both. Reread the prompt before submitting.
  • Going past 500 words. The seminar set a hard cap — don't overrun.
  • Over-cited names without context. Don't list "Boole, Frege, Hilbert, Gödel, Turing" without saying what each contributed.
